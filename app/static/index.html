Understanding PCOS (PCOD)

Plain-language facts based on the 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline for PCOS and WHO information.

What is PCOS?

Polycystic ovary syndrome is a common, lifelong hormone condition. It affects about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age. It involves some combination of:

  • Irregular or absent ovulation, which shows up as irregular, infrequent or missing periods.
  • Higher levels of androgens (“male-type” hormones that every woman has in small amounts), causing excess facial or body hair, acne or scalp hair thinning.
  • Many small follicles on the ovaries on ultrasound (or a high AMH blood level) in adults. These are not harmful cysts and do not need surgery.

PCOS or PCOD? In India, “PCOD” is widely used for the same symptoms. International guidelines and most doctors use “PCOS”, because it affects the whole body: metabolism, skin, mood and fertility, not just the ovaries.

Common signs

  • Periods more than 35 days apart, unpredictable, or absent
  • Dark, coarse hair on the face, chest, belly or thighs
  • Persistent acne
  • Thinning hair on the scalp
  • Weight gain, or finding it hard to lose weight
  • Dark, velvety skin patches (a sign of insulin resistance)
  • Difficulty getting pregnant
  • Low mood, anxiety or tiredness

PCOS in teenagers

Irregular periods are normal for the first year after the first period. Doctors use cut-offs based on the time since your first period:

  • 1–3 years after: cycles shorter than 21 or longer than 45 days
  • 3+ years after: shorter than 21 or longer than 35 days
  • Any time after year 1: a single cycle longer than 90 days
  • No period at all by age 15

Teens need both irregular cycles and signs of excess androgen to be diagnosed. Ultrasound is not used within 8 years of the first period. A teen with only one sign is “at risk” and should be checked again later.

How doctors diagnose it

Adults are diagnosed when 2 of these 3 are present (the Rotterdam criteria), after other causes are ruled out:

  1. Irregular cycles or ovulation problems
  2. Excess androgen: seen on the body (hair, acne, hair loss) or in blood tests
  3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound, or a high AMH level

Doctors also check thyroid function, prolactin and 17-hydroxyprogesterone, because these conditions can look like PCOS.

Why early diagnosis matters

  • Several times higher risk of type 2 diabetes and diabetes in pregnancy, so regular screening helps
  • Higher risk of high cholesterol and blood pressure
  • Very infrequent periods can thicken the womb lining; treatment protects it
  • More anxiety, depression and eating difficulties, and support works
  • Fertility problems are treatable. Most women with PCOS can have children.

Managing PCOS

Healthy lifestyle

Regular activity, balanced meals and good sleep improve insulin resistance, cycles and mood. With extra weight, losing 5–10% can restore ovulation. The aim is health, not a number on the scale.

Medical treatment

Depending on your goals, doctors may suggest the combined pill (cycles, acne, hair), metformin (metabolic health), letrozole (when trying to conceive), or anti-androgen and cosmetic treatments. Never self-medicate.

Emotional wellbeing

Feeling low, anxious or unhappy with your body is common with PCOS. Talking to a doctor, counsellor or trusted person really helps.

See a doctor soon if…

  • You have not had a period by age 15
  • You have gone 3 or more months without a period (and are not pregnant)
  • Your bleeding is very heavy, or lasts longer than 7 days
  • Hair growth is getting worse quickly, or your voice is deepening, as these need prompt checks
  • Your symptoms are affecting your confidence, studies, work or mood

Myths and facts

“PCOS means I can't get pregnant.”

False. Many women with PCOS conceive naturally. Others need help with ovulation, and treatments such as letrozole work well.

“Only overweight women get PCOS.”

False. PCOS happens at every body size. Lean women can have it too, though extra weight can make symptoms worse.

“The cysts must be removed by surgery.”

False. The “cysts” are small follicles (immature eggs), not harmful growths. PCOS is not treated by removing them.

“Irregular periods in my first year mean I have PCOS.”

False. Irregular cycles are expected in the first year after the first period while the body matures.

“The pill cures PCOS.”

Not quite. The pill manages symptoms such as irregular bleeding, acne and hair growth. PCOS is lifelong, but it is very manageable.
